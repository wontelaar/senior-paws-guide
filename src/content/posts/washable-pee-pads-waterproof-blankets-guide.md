A senior dog that starts having accidents in the house isn't being difficult, and it isn't "forgetting" its training. In most cases, the body is simply working against it. Arthritis and general joint stiffness make it harder to get up quickly, get down stairs, or hold a position long enough to signal that a trip outside is needed. Weakening bladder muscles, changes in kidney function, and cognitive decline can all shorten the window between "I need to go" and "I'm going." For a lot of older dogs, the gap between urge and accident simply isn't there anymore, no matter how well-trained they were at age three. Understanding this is the first step to responding with the right setup instead of frustration — the goal isn't to punish or retrain, it's to protect the floor, the furniture, and the dog's dignity while managing a physical reality.

That said, a new or worsening pattern of accidents in a senior dog is also something a veterinarian should hear about before it's chalked up to "just old age." Urinary tract infections, diabetes, kidney disease, and other treatable conditions can all show up first as house soiling, and some of them respond well to treatment. Pads and waterproof covers are there to manage the mess in the meantime — they're not a diagnosis, and they're not a substitute for finding out why the accidents are happening.

Once a vet visit is on the calendar (or has already ruled out anything urgent), it helps to think about the problem in terms of what surface you're protecting and how the dog will be positioned on it — this points to a few different approaches rather than one universal product.

## Approach two: waterproof blankets and bed covers

Pads solve the "protect this one spot" problem, but a lot of senior dogs don't stay in one spot — they migrate between a dog bed, the sofa, and a person's bed. For that situation, a waterproof blanket that can travel with the dog, or be draped over whatever surface it lands on, solves a different problem than a fixed pad does.

Ameritex Waterproof Dog Bed Cover Pet Blanket <a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B087BCGVS9?tag=seniorpawsgui-20" target="_blank" rel="nofollow sponsored noopener">Ameritex Waterproof Dog Bed Cover Pet Blanket</a> is built as a reversible cover meant for beds and sofas, and it's machine washable, which matters a great deal for a dog with regular accidents since it needs to go through the wash often without falling apart or losing its waterproofing. Because it's designed to drape over existing furniture rather than being a standalone pad, it's a good fit for households trying to protect a couch cushion or a bed comforter rather than a patch of floor. The trade-off worth thinking through is that a blanket-style cover isn't anchored in place the way a heavier pad might be, so it may need tucking or securing under a dog that shifts around a lot.

## Which approach actually fits your situation

Neither category is universally "better" — they answer different questions. A pad answers "where do I want messes to land instead of my floor," while a blanket answers "how do I protect whatever surface my dog happens to be lying on right now." Many households managing a senior dog with frequent accidents end up using both: a pad by the door or in a crate, and a blanket on the bed or couch where the dog actually spends its resting hours. The one thing to avoid regardless of which you choose is letting either product stay damp against the dog's skin. Urine left sitting against fur and skin can lead to irritation, sores, or infections, especially in a senior dog with already-thinning skin, so any wet pad or blanket should be swapped out and the dog dried off, not left "because the pad is absorbing it anyway."

## What to check before buying

- **Measure the actual resting spot** — the dog bed, the section of couch, or the floor space by the door — before ordering, since a pad or blanket that's too small will let accidents leak past the edges anyway.
- **Think about mobility, not just size.** A dog that struggles to stand may need a non-slip backing (like the silicone base on a pad) rather than a smooth-bottomed blanket that can shift under unsteady legs.
- **Check the care instructions on the specific listing** before assuming any product can go straight into a washing machine and dryer on normal settings — cycle and heat tolerance can affect waterproof layers over time.
- **Decide whether portability matters.** If vet visits, car rides, or travel are part of the picture, a foldable blanket may fit daily life better than a fixed floor pad.
- **Watch for skin contact time.** Whatever you choose, plan to check and change it promptly rather than letting a wet pad or blanket sit under the dog for hours.
- **Book a vet visit if accidents are new or increasing.** Ruling out a urinary infection, kidney issue, or other treatable cause should come before assuming this is simply old age.
